What we found, in plain terms

Across 17 snapshots from September 22 to October 10, 2026 (7,044 hotel prices in 368 cities, US market):

How often a cheaper listing exists

  • ✅ At 73% of 7,044 hotel prices some site was 5% or more below the going rate (61% to 81% in each snapshot)
  • ✅ Typically 16.7% below the going rate, about $23 a night; Super.com had the lowest listing most often (1,340 hotels)
  • ✅ At 1,930 hotels (27.4%), no site was 5% or more below the going rate

How often it names your room

  • ⚠️ At 4,294 of the 5,114 hotels with a lower listing, the lowest named no room
  • ⚠️ The lowest named a room at 820 of 7,044 (11.6%), which is not proof it is yours
  • ⚠️ A lower listing with a matching room name: 571 of 6,620 hotels (8.6%) on our original room check, in the 16 snapshots that compared room names

Why the big sites land close, and where they don't

It's structural, not coincidence. Hotels distribute rooms to the major channels at aligned rates, and rate parity agreements, contracts that discourage one public channel from undercutting another, plus competitive matching pull the public price together, though not all the way. The big OTAs also share corporate parents: Expedia, Hotels.com, Travelocity, and Orbitz are one company; Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, and Kayak are another. Much of what looks like comparison shopping is checking the same warehouse through different doors, which is why the price differences you do see are often about fees, currency, or a room the listing never names, rather than a provably cheaper room.

Parity also breaks in the wholesale layer. Hotels quietly release inventory to wholesale channels at lower rates to fill rooms, and resellers like Super.com or Algotels surface those prices publicly. Super.com had the lowest listing most often in our study (1,340 hotels, typically 18.4% below the going rate) and named the room at none; across all sites, the lowest listing named no room at 4,294 of the 5,114 hotels with a lower listing. Most of these listings name no room, so whether they buy the same room is usually unknown, which is exactly why they deserve verification rather than blind trust; we wrote up that class of booking in same room, different price. Opaque products push the same trade to its limit: with Priceline's Express Deals the discount is real, but even the hotel's name stays hidden until after you pay.

What this means for how you book

  • 1. Check more than one big brand. Expedia and Booking.com were within $2 a night at 1,797 of 3,056 hotels (59%) where both prices were on the same tax footing, in our 14 snapshots of September 25 to October 1, 2026; the lower all-in price is your baseline. More on the most-listed site: does Booking.com have the cheapest prices?
  • 2. When prices are equal, book direct. Loyalty points, easier changes, and better problem-handling are free when there's no price gap to give up.
  • 3. Treat wholesale discounts as leads. The lower prices can be real, but verify the room type, all-in total, and cancellation terms before paying.

Rate parity, common questions

Are hotel prices the same on every booking site?+

No. The big booking sites usually list about the same price: Expedia's lowest price was within 2% of the going rate at 91% of the hotels it listed and Booking.com's at 63%, in our 14 snapshots of September 25 to October 1, 2026. But some site was 5% or more below the going rate at 73% of the 7,044 hotel prices in our study (September 22 to October 10, 2026), usually a smaller site, and at 84% of those the lowest listing named no room.

So price comparison is pointless?+

No, it is just usually pointed at the wrong target. Even Booking.com and Expedia split: at 3,056 hotels where both prices were on the same tax footing, Expedia was more than 2% lower at 945 and Booking.com at 268. Comparing the going rate with lower listings, and all-in totals rather than headlines, is where real differences live. And comparing across time (watching for drops) often beats both.

Why did I once find the same room cheaper on another big site?+

It happens, but it is uncommon: in the 16 snapshots that compared room names, a listing 5% or more below the going rate with a matching room name turned up at 571 of 6,620 hotels (8.6%) on our original room check (952 of 5,830, or 16.3%, on the stricter October 1 check over the 14 snapshots of September 25 to October 1, 2026), and short-lived promotions, member pricing and currency effects create temporary gaps. A lower listing with a matching room name is the exception, not the rule, which is why we run the comparison on every hotel instead of assuming.

Do member prices and app-only deals break parity?+

Technically they sidestep it: closed-group rates (sign-in prices, app-only rates, opaque bookings) aren't public rates, so parity agreements don't cover them. They're usually modest discounts (around 10%) rather than the deep wholesale cuts, and they come with their own terms. Worth taking when they're on the room you already wanted.

How big was the study behind these numbers?+

7,044 priced hotels in 368 cities, in 17 snapshots from September 22 to October 10, 2026 (stays 21 days out in the first, 35 days out in the rest), US market, USD, every site Google Hotels lists. It replaces a much smaller earlier test, whose parity finding this data does not support. The headline held in every snapshot: a lower listing at 61% to 81% of hotels, the lowest naming a room at 5.9% to 20.4%. Each snapshot is one moment, not a census.
