Is Google Hotels accurate?
Mostly yes, with a few things to watch.

Google Hotels is a real, useful hotel metasearch, built right into Google Search and Google Maps. It doesn't sell rooms itself. It gathers rates that booking sites and hotels feed it, lines them up, and sends you off to one of them to book. The comparison is genuine and the rates are usually close, but the prices are supplied by partners and can lag, the most visible slots are a paid auction, and a headline rate can exclude taxes and fees until checkout. So the number it highlights isn't always the lowest all-in total. Here's the honest breakdown, and how to check whether a given price actually holds up.

How Google Hotels actually works

This is the part that trips people up, and the answer is mostly boring, not shady. Google Hotels is a metasearch, not a booking site. Booking sites and hotels feed it their rates, Google lines them up in the price list, and Google earns a fee when you click through to one of them. Because those clicks are how it gets paid, the most prominent slots in that price list are an auction that partners bid to occupy. It's a real price, but the order is partly driven by who paid, not purely by who's lowest.

That has three practical consequences. First, the rates are a snapshot that can lag, so a price can shift slightly between when Google fetched it and when you click. Second, the headline nightly rate can exclude taxes, resort fees, or currency conversion that only appear at the booking site's checkout, so the number you compare isn't always the number you pay. Third, Google hands you off to a booking site or the hotel to actually book, which means that site sets the cancellation terms, the support, and the final all-in charge, not Google. None of that makes it inaccurate. It just means the highlighted price is a starting point to verify, not a finish line. Standalone metasearch sites like Skyscanner work on the same handoff model.

Before you trust the highlighted Google Hotels rate, check three things

  • 1. The all-in total. Compare the price after taxes and fees, and in your own currency, not the headline nightly rate.
  • 2. The hotel's own site. Check it directly too. A member or loyalty rate can beat every OTA and sometimes isn't shared with Google.
  • 3. The cancellation terms. The lowest slot is often prepaid and non-refundable, which isn't worth a small difference if your plans might change.

At 40 of the 44, the page showed the price Google Hotels listed within 2%. The other 4:

  • Two Booking.com pages offered a signed-out visitor a members-only price 10.0% and 9.1% below Google's figure, beside a public price 24.5% and 26.1% above it.
  • A Priceline page showed $110 a night where Google listed $121 before tax, 9.1% lower.

Google does not always price for where you are. We ask Google Hotels for a visitor in the United States paying in US dollars, yet 296 of 838 priced answers (35%) on October 2 and 3, 2026 came back priced for a visitor in another country (most often Brazil, Canada and Britain), as the booking sites' own links say. Some sites price by country: on October 1, two Agoda offers priced for visitors in Vietnam and Venezuela showed our US browser 5.6% and 11.7% more before tax than the offer Google had.

So when we could read the page, it showed the price Google Hotels listed nearly every time. The exceptions to watch for are members-only prices, a price shown before tax, and an answer priced for another country.

How to check the price yourself

The honest way to know if a Google Hotels rate is really the lowest price is to compare it, the all-in total for the exact same room and dates, against the hotel's own site and one or two other booking sites, and to read the cancellation terms alongside the price. Doing that by hand across five tabs is tedious, which is exactly why most people don't bother and just click the highlighted number.

Google Hotels: common questions

Is Google Hotels legit and safe to use?+

Yes. It's Google's own hotel metasearch, built into Search and Maps, and the rates it shows come from genuine booking sites and hotels. The thing to understand is that Google doesn't process the booking itself, it sends you to a partner site to complete it, and that partner sets the terms and the final charge. Verify the all-in total and read the cancellation terms on the booking site and you avoid the most common surprises.

Why is the price higher once I click through to book?+

This is the most common surprise. The rate Google shows is a partner-fed snapshot that can lag, and the headline nightly figure can exclude taxes, resort fees, and currency conversion that only appear at the booking site's checkout, where that site, not Google, sets the final all-in charge. It usually isn't a bait price, it's an incomplete or slightly stale headline that fills in at checkout. Always check the full breakdown on the booking site so the price you approve is the price you pay.

Does the top result on Google Hotels mean it's the cheapest?+

Not necessarily. The most prominent slots in the price list are an auction that booking partners bid to occupy, so the order is partly driven by who paid to be there, not purely by who has the lowest all-in total. It's still a real rate, but treat the top slot as a starting point, compare the all-in totals for the same room across a couple of sites and the hotel direct before you decide.

Does Google Hotels miss some prices?+

Sometimes. In our price snapshots it listed a median of 17 booking sites per hotel, the hotel's own site at 66% of hotels, and never Hotwire, and a member or loyalty rate on the hotel's own site is sometimes not shared with Google at all. So check the hotel's own site too, and compare the totals with taxes and fees as each site reports them; a resort or destination fee may be extra.
