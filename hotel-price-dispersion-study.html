At 61% to 81% of hotels, some booking site listed the stay 5% or more below the going rate

We recorded 7,044 hotel prices in 368 cities on every booking site Google Hotels lists, in 17 snapshots from September 22 to October 10, 2026, each for a two-night stay. Pooled, 5,114 of them (73%) had a listing 5% or more below the going rate, the most common price among listings that name a room or offer free cancellation. Where room names were compared (14 snapshots), at 2,505 of the 4,181 hotels with such a listing (60%) none of those listings named a room, so check the room before you book. Each snapshot, the method and every CSV are below.

A stricter room check, October 1, 2026

Our original check compared only the single lowest-priced listing's room with the room at the going rate, and it counted loose matches (for example "Standard Room" with "Triple Room"). The new check is stricter about what counts as the same room name, and when the lowest-priced listing names no room it also checks the other listings 5% or more below the going rate.

Over the 14 snapshots of September 25 to October 1, 2026 that compared room names (5,830 priced hotels, 4,181 with a listing 5% or more below the going rate): the original check found a matching room name at 515 hotels (8.8%); 140 of those do not hold on the stricter check. The stricter check, looking at every listing 5% or more below, finds one at 952 hotels (16.3% of priced hotels, 22.8% of those with a cheaper listing). That is a floor: the stored rows keep one room pair per hotel.

At 2,505 of the 4,181 (59.9%), no listing 5% or more below the going rate named a room at all, so no room check is possible. A matching name is strong evidence, not proof: check the room before you book. The per-hotel CSVs keep the original check's column; the Underbook extension and price checker use the stricter check.

We then tested the stricter check on 300 fresh hotels with every room each site listed (October 1, method written down before the first search): of 194 with a listing 5% or more below the going rate, 53 (27%, likely between 22% and 34%) had one whose room name matched. A separate AI model, shown only the two room names, judged 42 of 50 sampled matches (84%) to be the same room, short of the 95% we set as the bar for calling a name match proof. So treat a matching name as a strong hint and check the room before you book. With the stricter rules in use since October 1, a second blind round on 186 fresh name matches from the October 2 repeat judged 167 (90%) the same room; most of the rest were judged unsure because one name says more than the other (a view or pool access), not a different room. Still short of 95%, so the advice stands.

Caveat, October 1, 2026: some prices were listed for travelers in other countries

Every search in this study asked Google Hotels for prices for a traveler in the US, in US dollars. The booking sites' links show that some answers came back priced for a visitor in another country instead (64 countries; the UK, Brazil and Canada most often). In the two runs that kept those links (October 1), 134 of 394 answers in the US-city run and 145 of 427 in the international run (34% of both) were priced for a visitor elsewhere. A site that prices by the visitor's country can show a US traveler a different price: of 6 such listings whose booking page we could read, 2 (Agoda) showed a higher price than ours, by 5.6% and 11.7%, and 4 showed our price.

Of the 578 hotels those two runs count as having a listing 5% or more below the going rate, 179 (31%) had such a listing as their lowest price, and for 156 of them no listing priced for a US traveler was left. Earlier runs did not keep the links, so they cannot be checked; they were searched the same way, and in a separate test of 300 hotels, 88 of 280 answers (31%) came back priced elsewhere. The headline, and the catch

For 331 of 424 hotels that had prices, some booking site listed the stay at least 5% under the most common price among listings that name a room or offer free cancellation. The typical gap was 19%, about $28 a night. That part of "the same hotel costs less somewhere else" is simply true.

The catch is what the cheaper listing is for. At 286 of those 331 hotels the lowest-priced listing named no room at all: the site publishes a price, not which room it buys. At only 45 of 424 (10.6%) did the lowest-priced listing name a room, and naming a room is not the same as being the same room: in a spot-check, some were a bed in a shared dorm priced against private rooms. They can still be real bargains. You just cannot know from the price alone, which is why our extension says "check the room" unless the room names match.

One more honesty number: for 31% of hotels, no site reported a before-tax rate at all, so the "all-in" price on screen may be missing resort or property fees. We flag those too.

Chains vs independents, and by region

We expected chains to be where the cheaper listing names its room, because chains publish room names everywhere. The data says the opposite. Chain hotels had a cheaper listing 82% of the time, but the lowest-priced listing named a room only 2.7% of the time; independents 77% and 13.5%. One possible reason, which this data cannot confirm: chains tend to keep their own site and the big booking sites at the same rate, so most cheaper listings come from wholesalers that publish a rate and no room; independents lean on Booking.com, Agoda and hostel sites, which carry room names (for hostels, often a dorm bed).

By region, a lowest-priced listing that named its room was most common in Europe (15.2% of hotels), then the US (9.0%), then Asia-Pacific (7.5%). A cheaper listing of some kind existed in 84% of US hotels, 82% in Asia-Pacific and 67% in Europe.

What to do with this when you book

  1. Assume a cheaper listing exists. It did for 78% of these hotels. The going rate is the most common price among the listings that name a room, not the floor.
  2. Before you pay less, confirm the room. Open the cheaper listing and check the room name, bed type and cancellation terms against the one you were about to book. If the site does not name the room, that is the answer.
  3. Check the all-in number. If no site shows a before-tax rate, resort or property fees may be missing from every price you see.
  4. If you are not booking today, watch it. Prices for the same stay move; a watch on one listing compares like with like and never needs a room match.

Underbook does steps 1 to 4 on the hotel page you are already on.

On a hotel page with your dates, it checks the other booking sites Google Hotels lists and shows the going rate, the lowest-priced listing, whether that listing names the same room (it says "check the room" when the names do not match), whether the prices include taxes and fees, and lets you watch the price by email. It sends the hotel name, the town, your dates and party size to our price service (privacy), and you book wherever you choose.

Method, limits and data

  • 426 hotels: the first 20 hotel-type properties Google Hotels returns for "hotels in city" in each of 25 cities (Amsterdam, Austin, Bali, Bangkok, Barcelona, Berlin, Chicago, Dubai, Honolulu, Las Vegas, Lisbon, London, Los Angeles, Miami, Nashville, New York, Orlando, Paris, Rome, San Francisco, Seoul, Singapore, Sydney, Tokyo, Vienna).
  • One snapshot per hotel on September 22, 2026, for a 2-night stay checking in 2026-10-13 and out 2026-10-15, 2 adults, US market, USD. A snapshot is not a forecast: prices move and this is one day.
  • "Going rate" is the rate the largest number of credible listings agree on, with ties broken low. "Cheaper listing" is any site 5% or more below it. "Named a room" means the lowest-priced site published a room name for its rate. These are the rules the Underbook extension applied live on September 22; the study reused its code rather than a copy.
  • Prices come from Google Hotels' listing of each booking site. Sites not listed there are not in the data. A site with the lowest price was lowest among what was listed that day.
  • "Chain" means the hotel's name matched a list of chain brands fixed before the run (Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, IHG, Accor, Wyndham, Choice and others); every other hotel counts as independent. It is a name rule, so a few chain hotels without the brand in their name count as independent.
  • Full per-hotel table: CSV download (CC BY 4.0, cite Underbook). The CSV lists before-tax nightly rates, and sites_listed counts the listing entries kept in the table, the 12 lowest-priced at most (a site listed twice counts twice); the going rate and the lowest price use every listing. The hotel pages show the same stays all-in (taxes and fees as the sites reported them) and every site found, so their dollar figures are higher and their site counts larger. The discount percentages usually agree within a few points, but for some hotels they differ by more, and a hotel near the 5% line can land on different sides of it, so compare like with like.
