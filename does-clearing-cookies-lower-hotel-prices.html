The verdict, in plain terms

Clearing cookies is harmless to try, but it is not a reliable way to pay less. The price differences people notice are real, they just do not come from your browser history.

What the cookie trick can't do

  • ⚠️ Sites don't reliably punish you for looking twice

Why the myth feels true

The reason this tip spreads is that people really do see prices change. You check a room on Monday, look again Wednesday, and the number is different, so it feels like the site is watching you and nudging the price up. The mistake is in the cause, not the observation. Hotel rooms use dynamic pricing: the rate moves with live demand, how many rooms are left, the day of week, the season, and sometimes short-lived price tests. All of that shifts on its own, whether or not you ever visited before.

The one controlled test we can point to is a 2014 Northeastern University study (Hannak et al., IMC 2014). It ran matched browsers side by side on six booking sites, including one that cleared its cookies after every search. That browser was never shown a lower price. On Expedia and Hotels.com, clearing cookies dropped it into a different A/B test group, which changed which hotels were listed and in what order, not what they cost. The lower prices it did find went the other way: signed-in members on Orbitz and Cheaptickets saw about $12 off on about 5% of hotels, and iOS users on Travelocity about $15 off on about 5%. The study is from 2014, so treat it as evidence, not a promise about today; we have not run a cookie test ourselves.

The part that actually costs you money

A private window signs you out. Many booking sites and hotel loyalty programs show lower member or app-only rates only when you are logged in. So the cookie-clearing ritual can quietly do the opposite of what you wanted, hiding a discount you had already earned. If anything, staying signed in is the move, not scrubbing your session.

What actually lowers the price

The ways to pay less that hold up are boring and repeatable. The biggest one is simply comparing the all-in total for the same room and dates across sites. The same hotel often shows different totals on the hotel's own site, on Booking, on Expedia and on the resellers on the same day, because each negotiates and marks up differently. The catch is that the lowest-priced listing often does not say which room it is; when the room checks out, that gap is real money, and it has nothing to do with your cookies.

After that: be flexible on dates when you can, since a shift of one night can cross a demand threshold; sign in so you see member and mobile rates; and watch the price over time so you book on a dip instead of the first number you saw. Those four levers beat every incognito trick, because they act on the thing that really sets the price rather than on a myth about being tracked.

Cookies and hotel prices: common questions

Does clearing cookies actually lower hotel prices?+

Usually not in any reliable way. In the one controlled test we know of (Northeastern University, 2014), clearing cookies never produced a lower hotel price on six booking sites. When the number does change, it is almost always live demand, availability, currency, or logging out of a member rate, rather than a site personally marking you up. Try it if you like, but do not count on it to lower the price.

Does incognito mode make hotels cheaper?+

Not reliably, and it can backfire. A private window logs you out, so you can lose signed-in member and app-only prices that were genuinely lower. The occasional cheaper number in incognito is usually normal price movement that would have appeared anyway. Use it as a quick sanity check, not a strategy.

Do sites raise the price if I keep checking the same room?+

There is no solid evidence they quietly punish you for revisiting. Prices move while you shop because of dynamic pricing tied to real demand and shrinking availability, plus occasional A/B tests. The price can drop between visits just as easily as it rises. What feels like being tracked is almost always the room simply getting more or less available.

So what actually makes a hotel room cheaper?+

Compare the all-in total, after taxes and fees, for the same room and dates across the hotel's own site and a couple of booking sites; be flexible on dates when you can; sign in for member and app-only rates; and watch the price so you book on a dip. The comparison step is the biggest lever, because the same hotel is often listed for less somewhere else on the same day.
